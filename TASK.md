# TASK: AI took a task, not the job — the radiology case, and three controls

Keyword: **TASK**

> Resource for the video "The job AI was supposed to replace averaged $520,000 last year, per Works in Progress." Jonathan / Ai.SystemsByJon, 18 Nov 2026.

A one-page setup for business owners who are thinking about giving an AI agent a role. It is based on one article, about one regulated job. Not a prediction about your industry, and not medical, legal, or business advice.

**The article (checked 18 Nov 2026, America/Caracas):** Deena Mousa, «[AI isn't replacing radiologists](https://www.worksinprogress.news/p/why-ai-isnt-replacing-radiologists)», *Works in Progress*, 25 Sep 2025. That is the only source used here. If you publish this later than 2026, change "last year" to "in 2025."

## What the article reports (verified against that page)

- **In 2025**, radiology was the second-highest-paid medical specialty in the country, **with an average income of $520,000**. Not "every radiologist makes $520,000."
- **In 2016**, computer scientist Geoffrey Hinton «declared that 'people should stop training radiologists now'.» The article does not, in the lines we used, say "the first job AI will destroy" or "within five years," and we did not verify a retraction.
- There are **over 700 FDA-cleared radiology models**, «which account for roughly three-quarters of all medical AI devices.» That is **FDA-cleared**, not "FDA-approved to replace doctors," and not a claim that every hospital uses them.
- **In 2025**, American diagnostic radiology residency programs offered **a record 1,208 positions** across all radiology specialties. That is residency positions, not "1,208 new jobs," and not "all residency programs."
- Pay is **over 48 percent higher than the average salary in 2015**. The article does not say AI caused the raise. It also uses "48 percent" in another context (AI use); don't mix those figures.
- **A 2012 study** of staff radiologists in **three hospitals** found that **only 36 percent of their time** was dedicated to direct image interpretation. That is not a current national figure. Keep the year.
- On out-of-sample tests, «The performance of a tool can drop as much as **20 percentage points** when it is tested out of sample, on data from other hospitals.» That is a maximum in that hospital data, not "AI is 20% worse," and not a number to paste onto business agents.

The article's own wording is that **models replace only a small share of a radiologist's job**. My rule is stronger than that sentence, and it is **not a quotation** from the article:

**AI took a task. Not the job.**

Don't read that as "AI can't replace jobs" in general. The article itself is about this profession, and it notes the paradox has held "for now."

Pick one repeatable task with a clear finish line. Don't hand over the whole job title.

### 2. Test it on your own data.

The article's warning is about tools that look strong on one hospital's data and then drop by as much as 20 percentage points on another hospital's data.

- [ ] What sample of **your** data will you use for the test?
- [ ] What will you write down (accuracy, errors, time, what it missed)?
- [ ] Did it hold up on data it wasn't tuned on?

### 3. A person signs off on the result.

- [ ] Who signs off?
- [ ] How often (every result at first, then a sample)?
- [ ] What must not go out without that signature (customer messages, money, records)?

If nobody has time to review, the agent isn't ready for that task.

## Quick check

- [ ] Did you list the tasks, or did you assign a job title?
- [ ] Is only **one** task marked for the AI?
- [ ] Did you test it on your own data, and write down what happened?
- [ ] Is a named person signing off on the result?
